# PRIMARY OBJECTIVE

Find a proof of one of the following:

### A. Positive density

Prove that there exist constants (c>0) and (N_0) such that

[
F(N):=|A\cap[1,N]|\ge cN
]

for every (N\ge N_0).

This would prove positive lower density and, combined with appropriate upper/lower arguments, potentially resolve GREEN-036.

### B. Zero density

Prove rigorously that

[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{F(N)}N=0.
]

### C. Density does not exist

Prove that (F(N)/N) has at least two different subsequential limits.

### D. Exact reduction

Reduce GREEN-036 to another explicit theorem whose proof would immediately resolve the problem.

Anything weaker remains **OPEN**.

# CENTRAL STRUCTURE TO EXPLOIT

The crucial exact identity is

[
a\in A\quad\Longrightarrow\quad aA-1\subseteq A.
]

Equivalently, for every (a\in A),

[
T_a(A)\subseteq A,
\qquad
T_a(x)=ax-1.
]

Also,

[
n\in A,\ n>1
\iff
\exists,a,b\in A:\ n+1=ab.
]

The existing mean-field model failed because these images are strongly correlated.

Therefore, investigate the **deterministic self-affine structure**, not an independence heuristic.

# HIGHEST-PRIORITY ATTACK

Try to prove a genuine density-bootstrap theorem.

For example, investigate whether there exist functions (G,\Phi) such that a lower-density statement at scale (N) forces a stronger statement at a larger scale:

[
F(N)\ge\delta N
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
F(CN)\ge \Phi(\delta),CN.
]

A particularly valuable result would be one where

[
\Phi(\delta)>\delta
]

for every (0<\delta<2/3), or at least for some interval of positive (\delta).

Try to determine whether repeated application forces a positive limiting density.

Do not assume such a bootstrap exists: either prove it or find a rigorous obstruction.

# INVESTIGATE THESE ROUTES

## 1. Covering by affine images

Study

[
\bigcup_{a\in A}(aA-1).
]

Can this union be shown to have positive density?

Estimate overlaps rigorously.

Do not use probabilistic independence.

Try to obtain deterministic inequalities for

[
\left|\bigcup_{a\in B}(aA-1)\cap[1,N]\right|
]

for finite (B\subseteq A).

---

## 2. Complement argument

Let

[
B=\mathbb N\setminus A.
]

Characterize non-membership through

[
n\in B
\iff
n+1=ab
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a\in B\text{ or }b\in B
]

for every divisor pair (a,b).

Investigate whether a complement of positive density is compatible with this recursive condition.

Try to prove a contradiction from

[
\overline d(B)>0.
]

A successful argument could immediately imply positive density of (A).

---

## 3. Interval methods

Search for a theorem that transforms a dense subset of an interval into a denser set at a larger scale using

[
x\mapsto ax-1.
]

For example, investigate statements resembling:

[
|A\cap[1,N]|\ge\delta N
]

forcing many values in intervals of the form

[
[aN-c,aN]
]

or unions of such intervals.

The critical goal is to turn multiplicative closure into **linear-scale coverage**.

---

## 4. Divisor structure of (n+1)

Use the exact criterion

[
n\in A
\iff
\exists d\mid n+1
\text{ such that }
d,\frac{n+1}{d}\in A.
]

Study whether integers (n+1) with sufficiently many divisors necessarily produce membership.

Look for deterministic statements such as:

> sufficiently many suitable divisor pairs force (n\in A).

Then connect divisor abundance to positive density.

---

## 5. Iterated transformations

Study compositions

[
T_a(x)=ax-1.
]

For (a,b\in A),

[
T_a(T_b(x))
===========

abx-a-1.
]

Investigate whether the semigroup generated by these affine transformations produces enough residue/interval coverage to imply density.

Look for exact algebraic structure in the transformation semigroup.

---

## 6. Additive and multiplicative combinatorics

Investigate whether known sum-product or product-set phenomena can be applied to subsets of (A).

In particular, if

[
|A\cap[1,N]|
]

is already a positive fraction of (N), what can be proved about

[
AA={ab:a,b\in A}?
]

Since

[
AA-1\subseteq A,
]

any sufficiently strong deterministic result about (AA) could potentially bootstrap density.

Do not cite a generic sum-product theorem unless all hypotheses match exactly.

---

## 7. Larger modular / (p)-adic structure

The existing search through modulus (500) found only the mod-(3) obstruction.

Do not merely repeat the same search.

Instead ask whether there are structural restrictions involving:

* valuations (v_p(n+1));
* simultaneous congruences;
* (p)-adic neighborhoods;
* residue restrictions conditioned on membership of factors;
* infinitely many moduli;
* non-periodic congruence constraints.

A new restriction is useful only if it yields a quantitative density theorem.
